Energy & Health

High energy all day while training hard comes from sleep, light, movement, fuel and recovery. Here’s what the research says about each, and a daily routine built on it.

Daily routine Sleep Caffeine Movement Cardio Recovery Checkups Sources

A high-energy day

WakeSame time every day, weekends included. Water, then outside within the first hour — daylight, even on cloudy days, moves your body clock earlier.[1]
MorningProtein-heavy breakfast.[2] Caffeine now, if you use it. Short flexibility routine if there’s time.
MiddayProtein, vegetables and some carbs. Walk for a few minutes after eating.[3] Last caffeine by early afternoon.[4]
TrainingCarbs and protein in the 1–2 hours before or after.[5] Earlier in the day protects sleep.
EveningDim the lights and screens — evening indoor light pushes your body clock later.[1] Wind-down routine. Room cool, dark and quiet.
BedTime in bed long enough for 7–9 hours of actual sleep.[6]

Sleep

  • 7 or more hours a night is the expert consensus for adults.[6]
  • Sleep decides what you lose when dieting. In a study where dieters ate the same calories, those sleeping 5.5 hours lost far less fat and far more lean mass than those sleeping 8.5 — about 25% of the weight lost was fat versus about 56%.[7]
  • Consistency beats catch-up. A fixed wake time and daytime light anchor your body clock.[1]
  • Persistent trouble sleeping, loud snoring or waking unrefreshed are worth raising with a doctor. The Sleep Solution is a practical, doctor-written guide.

Light

Daylight sets your body clock. In a University of Washington study of 507 students, each hour of daytime light moved the body clock about 30 minutes earlier — even overcast light counted — while each hour of evening indoor light pushed it about 15 minutes later.[1] Get outside in the morning and around midday; keep evenings dim.

Caffeine

  • It works: 3–6 mg/kg about an hour before training improves strength and endurance.[8]
  • In a sleep-lab study, 400 mg taken 6 hours before bed still cut sleep by over an hour — and people didn’t notice.[4]
  • Rule of thumb: nothing within 8–10 hours of bedtime. For a 10 pm bedtime, that’s noon to 2 pm.
  • If you rely on it to function, cut back for a week or two; it works better when you’re not dependent on it.

Daily movement

  • Steps: in a meta-analysis of 15 studies, the death-risk benefit leveled off at about 8,000–10,000 steps a day for adults under 60. Compared with about 3,500 steps, more active groups had 40–53% lower risk.[9]
  • Walk after meals: even 2–5 minutes of light walking after eating smooths the blood-sugar rise; light walking breaks cut glucose about 17% versus sitting.[3]
  • Break up sitting every 20–30 minutes when you can — stand, walk, a few squats.[3]

Cardio

  • Guidelines: 150–300 minutes of moderate activity a week, plus muscle strengthening on 2+ days.[10]
  • Fitness and lifespan: in 122,000 treadmill-tested adults, the least fit had about 5 times the death risk of the fittest, with no upper limit to the benefit. The biggest gain comes from leaving the bottom group.[11]
  • It won’t kill your gains. A 2022 meta-analysis found cardio didn’t reduce whole-muscle growth; running interfered more than cycling.[12]
  • Plan: 2–3 sessions of 30–45 minutes at a conversational pace (bike, incline walk, rucking), plus an optional short interval session. Keep hard leg cardio away from leg days, or do it after lifting.

Recovery & overtraining

  • In a survey of 605 strength athletes, the most common sign of overdoing it was general fatigue, often with aches and an unexplained drop in performance. Over 90% of cases also involved stress outside the gym.[13]
  • Weekly check: dreading training, sleeping worse, numbers falling, aches building? Two or more — take a lighter week.[14]
  • Life stress counts as training stress. In hard seasons, cut volume rather than sleep.[13]
  • A full day of rest each week.

Checkups & things to track

Ask about at a yearly physical

  • CBC — blood counts; CMP — kidneys, liver, electrolytes, fasting glucose[15]
  • Lipid panel and A1C — heart and blood-sugar risk[15]
  • Vitamin D, iron/ferritin, thyroid — worth checking if you’re tired without explanation[15]
  • Blood pressure every visit

Tell the doctor you lift: hard training can raise liver enzymes on a lab report,[16] and creatine raises creatinine without harming the kidneys.[17]

Track at home

  • Morning weight (weekly average) and waist measurement monthly
  • Main lifts and the checkpoints from the Flexibility page
  • Sleep hours and how rested you feel, 1–5
