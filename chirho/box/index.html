Ray study 4 of 4 (from v17) - Jeweled glory: v17's alternating rays with a jewel at the tip of every straight ray.

  • Precedent: Baroque monstrances whose rays end in stones or stars - the glory 'set' like a piece of jewelry.
  • Same rays as v17; each straight ray ends in a bead, larger on the long rays and smaller on the short ones, making a beaded ring just inside the lettering.
  • Classical read: the beads close the composition - the rays stop at a defined edge instead of fading out.
  • Modern read: the bead ring doubles as a frame and gives the eye a place to rest; the richest of the four without getting heavier near the glyph.

v20 2026-09-25

Ray study 3 of 4 (from v17) - Tapered glory: slim filled rays that swell outward, alternating long and short, with hairlines between.

  • Precedent: the cast and chased rays of silver-gilt monstrances and Art Deco sunbursts - solid rays that taper to a point at the center.
  • 24 filled rays up to 1.2pt wide at the rim, alternating long and short, with 24 hairlines between that start 8pt out.
  • Classical read: the most sculptural - it has real light and shade, like metalwork.
  • Modern read: the boldest ground; the solid rays compete most with the glyph, so they are kept slim. Strong at a distance, the likeliest to feel heavy in hand.

v19 2026-09-25

Ray study 2 of 4 (from v17) - Jesuit glory: straight rays gathered in bundles of three with a flame between each bundle.

  • Precedent: the IHS sunburst of the Society of Jesus and Counter-Reformation altar glories, where rays are grouped in sheaves with flames between them.
  • 16 sheaves of three straight rays (a long center ray flanked by two shorter ones) alternating with wavy flame rays.
  • Classical read: a stronger rhythm than even spacing - the eye counts sheaves, not lines, which reads as richer without adding clutter.
  • Modern read: groupings give the ground a clear structure; still more texture than v18. Sixteen sheaves divide evenly around the glyph's four arms.

v18 2026-09-25

Ray study 1 of 4 (from v17) - Sun in splendour: straight rays only, in three lengths.

  • Precedent: the heraldic 'sun in splendour' (the badge of Edward III and later English kings) and medieval halos drawn as plain radiating lines.
  • 72 straight rays in a long-short-medium-short rhythm. Only the long rays reach the center jewel; the shorter ones start further out so the middle stays crisp.
  • Classical read: order through repetition - one stroke type, regular rhythm, perfect radial symmetry.
  • Modern read: the calmest of the ray studies; straight lines echo the glyph's own straight arms, so figure and ground feel like one drawing.

v16 revised: the rays now run in to the center jewel, and the clearance around the glyph is tighter.

  • Straight rays start at the edge of the center jewel (5.2pt out); the wavy rays start 10pt out so the middle doesn't clog.
  • Clearance around the glyph's strokes is down from 2.8pt to 1.5pt, so the rays read as passing behind the glyph rather than stopping short of it.
  • Classical read: rays converging on the crossing make the center unmistakable - the monstrance's whole point.
  • Modern read: denser toward the middle, so the glyph sits on a mid-tone rather than open paper; the engraved inline keeps it legible.

v16 2026-09-25

Study 4 of 4 (from v11) - Baroque: a glory of rays from the crossing. Badge only; rest of the box as v11.

  • Precedent: 17th-century sunburst monstrances, whose rays alternate straight (the light that strikes) and wavy (the warmth that envelops).
  • 56 rays from the crossing, held 2.8pt clear of the glyph and kept out of a 15pt halo around the boss, so the center stays calm.
  • Classical read: the strongest hierarchy of the four - every line points to the center - and the drama suits the subject.
  • Modern read: behind a line glyph the rays become texture and reduce figure-ground contrast; the most period-costume of the set. Rays are 0.4pt and will print as a soft tone.

v14 2026-09-25

Study 2 of 4 (from v11) - Roman: a laurel wreath crowns the Chi Rho, flanked by martyrs' palms. Whole-box redesign in laurel.

  • Precedent: the Passion Sarcophagus (Vatican, c. 350) encircles the Chi Rho with a laurel wreath - the Roman victory crown recast as a sign of resurrection. Palms are the martyr's victory branch on countless early Christian epitaphs.
  • The wreath (two branches tied with a ribbon bow at the foot) fills the band where the thin circle was; palm fronds fill the big side ovals, clipped clear of the glyph.
  • Whole box: laurel corners, laurel rules tied with a bow above and below the badge, laurel sprigs on both side labels and around the bottom logo.
  • Classical read: unity in variety - one motif family carried through every panel - and decorum: victory iconography suits a deck meant to be won by memorizing.
  • Modern read: a coherent system with a clear story, but more texture; at arm's length the wreath reads as a grey frame, which is its job. Alternating leaves keep the straight rules from reading as arrow fletching.

v13 2026-09-25

Study 1 of 4 (from v11) - Early Christian: Alpha and Omega flank the Chi Rho. Badge only; rest of the box as v11.

  • Precedent: the bronze coins of Magnentius (353) set the Chi Rho between Alpha and omega, and the pairing runs through catacomb epitaphs after Revelation 1:8, 'I am Alpha and Omega'.
  • Fills only the two largest open spaces (the big side ovals). The small pockets are left empty on purpose.
  • Classical read: decorum and iconography - every added mark carries meaning; strict bilateral symmetry; the glyph stays the single focal point.
  • Modern read: passes the Loos test (no ornament without a job); keeps the most negative space and the clearest figure-ground; strongest at thumbnail size and from across a room.
  • Watch: the letters can compete with the glyph's weight, so they are held to about 18pt tall and centered in each space by its largest inscribed circle.
