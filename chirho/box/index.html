v31 with a hairline paper outline: the white gap around the glyph and jewel is 0.5pt instead of 1.5pt.

  • 0.5pt is about two pixels at 300dpi print resolution - just enough for the rays to read as passing behind the glyph without a visible halo.
  • Rays now come almost to the glyph's edge, so the ray field reads as nearly continuous and the glyph as laid directly on it.

v25 2026-09-25

Field study 4 of 4 (from v17) - Azure hatching: the field engraved in fine horizontal lines, the glyph left in reserve.

  • Precedent: the Petra Sancta hatching system (1638) that engravers use to show heraldic colors in black and white - horizontal lines mean azure, blue - as on engraved seals, bookplates and coins.
  • Horizontal hairlines 1.75pt apart fill the circle; the glyph sits in a 1.5pt paper outline, as if cut from the engraving.
  • Classical read: heraldically correct and learned - the field literally says 'blue' in the engraver's code, matching the box's ink.
  • Modern read: the most restrained and graphic of the four - a single texture, even tone, very clean edges; reads like a stamp or seal and holds up well at small sizes.

v24 2026-09-25

Field study 3 of 4 (from v17) - Gothic diaper: a lozenge lattice with a quatrefoil in each lozenge.

  • Precedent: the diapered grounds behind figures in 13th-14th century manuscripts (the Westminster Psalter, the Parisian workshops) and heraldic diapering, used to enliven a flat field.
  • A 45-degree lattice centered on the crossing, a bead at every intersection and a quatrefoil in every lozenge; lattice lines run behind the glyph, but no quatrefoil is ever cut by it.
  • Classical read: textile-like, orderly and devotional; the grid gives the badge a clear sense of structure and craft.
  • Modern read: the grid is legible and rational (a quiet nod to modernist grids), but its diagonals fight the chi's diagonals; strong pattern, busier figure-ground than the stars.

v23 2026-09-25

Field study 2 of 4 (from v17) - Starry vault: rings of eight-pointed stars around the glyph.

  • Precedent: the vault of the Mausoleum of Galla Placidia in Ravenna (c. 430), where a gold cross hangs in a deep blue sky of stars set in rings that grow toward the edge.
  • Stars in concentric rings around the crossing, alternate rings offset, growing from 1.2pt near the jewel to 2.3pt at the rim; any star that would touch the glyph is left out rather than cut.
  • Classical read: iconographically the most direct - the sign of Christ set in the heavens - and the growth toward the rim gives depth, like looking up into a dome.
  • Modern read: the most open of the four; the stars are separate marks, so paper shows between them and figure-ground stays clear. The one most likely to please both camps.

v22 2026-09-25

Field study 1 of 4 (from v17) - Guilloche: an engine-turned rosette fills the circle behind the glyph.

  • Precedent: rose-engine guilloche - the machine-turned lace on Breguet watch dials, Faberge enamels and banknote rosettes, where it doubled as protection against forgery.
  • Bands of phase-shifted waves that just touch, from the center jewel to the rim, with petals kept about the same size in every band so the lace reads as one surface.
  • Classical read: order through mathematics - perfectly regular, endlessly repeating, and precious-looking; it makes the box feel like an heirloom or a certificate.
  • Modern read: the densest ground of the four, a true mid-tone; the glyph holds because of its paper halo, but at thumbnail size the field reads as a grey disc. Lines are 0.26pt - the finest on the box, so proof it.

v14 2026-09-25

Study 2 of 4 (from v11) - Roman: a laurel wreath crowns the Chi Rho, flanked by martyrs' palms. Whole-box redesign in laurel.

  • Precedent: the Passion Sarcophagus (Vatican, c. 350) encircles the Chi Rho with a laurel wreath - the Roman victory crown recast as a sign of resurrection. Palms are the martyr's victory branch on countless early Christian epitaphs.
  • The wreath (two branches tied with a ribbon bow at the foot) fills the band where the thin circle was; palm fronds fill the big side ovals, clipped clear of the glyph.
  • Whole box: laurel corners, laurel rules tied with a bow above and below the badge, laurel sprigs on both side labels and around the bottom logo.
  • Classical read: unity in variety - one motif family carried through every panel - and decorum: victory iconography suits a deck meant to be won by memorizing.
  • Modern read: a coherent system with a clear story, but more texture; at arm's length the wreath reads as a grey frame, which is its job. Alternating leaves keep the straight rules from reading as arrow fletching.

Study 1 of 4 (from v11) - Early Christian: Alpha and Omega flank the Chi Rho. Badge only; rest of the box as v11.

  • Precedent: the bronze coins of Magnentius (353) set the Chi Rho between Alpha and omega, and the pairing runs through catacomb epitaphs after Revelation 1:8, 'I am Alpha and Omega'.
  • Fills only the two largest open spaces (the big side ovals). The small pockets are left empty on purpose.
  • Classical read: decorum and iconography - every added mark carries meaning; strict bilateral symmetry; the glyph stays the single focal point.
  • Modern read: passes the Loos test (no ornament without a job); keeps the most negative space and the clearest figure-ground; strongest at thumbnail size and from across a room.
  • Watch: the letters can compete with the glyph's weight, so they are held to about 18pt tall and centered in each space by its largest inscribed circle.
