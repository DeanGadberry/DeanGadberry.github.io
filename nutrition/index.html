Nutrition & Macros

What to eat to build muscle, lean out and keep your energy up — with a macro calculator, the research behind the numbers, and three ways to track.

Priorities Calculator Protein Tracking Supplements Resources Sources

What matters, in order

  1. Calories — decide whether you gain, lose or hold weight.[1]
  2. Protein — decides how much of that weight is muscle.[2]
  3. Carbs & fat — carbs fuel hard training; fat supports hormones. Split the rest of your calories to taste.
  4. Food quality — fiber, fruit and vegetables, micronutrients.
  5. Meal timing — spread protein across the day; eat around training.
  6. Supplements — a small finishing touch.

The order follows Eric Helms’ Muscle and Strength Pyramid: Nutrition.

Rates of change

Lean bulk10–20% above maintenance, gaining 0.25–0.5% of bodyweight per week. The more experienced you are, the slower you should gain.[1]
CutLose 0.5–1% of bodyweight per week. Faster than that risks muscle and energy.[5]
RecompEating near maintenance with high protein can build muscle and lose fat at once — best for newer lifters or those returning after a break.[1]

Protein

  • Daily: 1.6–2.2 g/kg (0.7–1 g/lb). A large 2018 meta-analysis found muscle gains leveled off around 1.6 g/kg; 2.2 covers nearly everyone.[6, 1]
  • While cutting: aim higher — 2.3–3.1 g per kg of lean mass helps keep muscle.[5]
  • Per meal: about 0.4–0.55 g/kg (roughly 30–50 g) across 3–6 meals, every 3–4 hours.[1, 2]
  • Before bed: 30–40 g of slow protein (Greek yogurt, cottage cheese, casein) raises overnight muscle building.[2]
  • Best sources: meat, fish, eggs, dairy, whey. Plant sources work in slightly larger amounts.[2]

Carbs, fat & fiber

  • Fat: 0.5–1.5 g/kg (about 0.25–0.7 g/lb). Don’t drop below ~15–20% of calories for long.[1, 5]
  • Carbs: the remaining calories, at least 3–5 g/kg when training hard. Put a good share before and after training.[1]
  • Fiber: 14 g per 1,000 calories — about 38 g for most men. Fewer than 1 in 10 adults get there.[7]
  • Fruit and vegetables at most meals; oily fish twice a week.
  • Alcohol blunts muscle building: a heavy dose after training cut muscle protein synthesis by about 24–37%. Keep it occasional and small.[8]

Three ways to track

1. Full macro tracking

Best for
Learning portions, cutting for a deadline, breaking a stall.
How
A kitchen scale; weigh food raw; log as you eat, not at night; save recipes and repeat meals so logging takes minutes. Hit protein and calories — carbs and fat can float.

2. Hand portions (no counting)

Best for
Long stretches of maintenance or lean bulking without an app.
How
Per meal, about 4 meals a day: 2 palms of protein, 2 fists of vegetables, 2 cupped hands of carbs, 2 thumbs of fat. Gaining too slowly? Add a cupped hand of carbs or a thumb of fat to a meal or two. Stalled on a cut? Remove one.[9]
Guide
Precision Nutrition hand-portion guide (PDF)

3. Protein + scale only

Best for
The simplest system that still works.
How
Count only protein, weigh in daily, and nudge portions based on the weekly average.

Tools

  • Cronometer — food data from USDA and NCCDB, tracks dozens of vitamins and minerals, free tier.[4] Also logs from a desktop browser, so it works from a laptop.[10]
  • MacroFactor — phone app from the Stronger By Science team; adjusts your targets weekly from your intake and weight trend. Paid (about $72/year).[4]
  • MyFitnessPal — a huge food database, but many entries are user-submitted; double-check them.[11]
  • USDA FoodData Central — the government database to check any food.
  • A spreadsheet or notebook works too: date, morning weight, calories, protein.

Eating for steady energy

  • Protein and fiber at every meal keep you full and blood sugar steady.[2, 7]
  • Eat carbs before and after training;[1] a huge, mostly-carb lunch is a common cause of the afternoon slump.
  • Even a 2–5 minute walk after meals blunts the blood-sugar spike.[12]
  • Drink water through the day; around hard training, add salt or electrolytes.
  • A deep, long deficit is the biggest energy killer — take a week or two at maintenance every 8–12 weeks of cutting.[13]

Supplements

Worth it Creatine monohydrate — 3–5 g every day, any time. The most effective legal supplement for strength and muscle; safe in long-term studies.[14, 15]
Caffeine — 3–6 mg/kg about an hour before training improves performance.[16] Mind the cutoff time (see Energy).
Protein powder — food, just convenient.
If needed Vitamin D if blood levels are low or sun is scarce; fish oil if you rarely eat fish; electrolytes for heavy sweating.
MinorBeta-alanine (3–5 g/day), citrulline malate (~8 g pre-workout) — small effects.[1]
SkipBCAAs (pointless if protein is adequate), “test boosters,” fat burners, proprietary blends.
