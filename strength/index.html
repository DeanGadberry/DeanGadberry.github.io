What the research agrees on

Programs look different on the surface, but the good ones share the same core.

Volume 10–20 hard sets per muscle per week covers most people. A 2025 meta-analysis of 67 studies found growth from as little as 4 weekly sets, with more sets still helping but each extra set adding less — there’s little data above about 25.
Effort Stopping closer to failure builds more muscle; strength gains are similar across a wide range of effort. Practical rule: big compound lifts 1–3 reps short of failure, isolation lifts 0–2.
Frequency Each muscle about twice a week. Split a muscle’s weekly sets across days once you pass roughly 10.
Reps Anything from about 5 to 30 reps builds muscle if taken close to failure. Heavier sets (1–6) are better for strength; use 6–15 for most hypertrophy work.
Progression Add reps, then weight. Double progression: e.g. 3 sets of 8–12 — once every set hits 12, add weight and drop back to 8.
Range Full range of motion, with emphasis on the stretched position (deep squats, seated leg curls, deep-stretch flyes). A 2026 review found long-muscle-length training may beat short-length training for growth, though the evidence is still mixed.
Rest2–3 minutes for compounds, 1–2 for isolation.
Deloads Reactive rather than scheduled. Each week, ask: dreading training? sleeping worse? loads or reps falling? aches worse than usual? Two or more yeses — take a lighter week.

Pelland et al., Sports Medicine 2025 (volume) · Robinson et al., Sports Medicine 2024 (proximity to failure) · Wolf et al., Sports Medicine and Health Science 2026 (muscle length) · Andy Morgan & Eric Helms, RippedBody (deload check)

Schools of thought

Each one is a different way of applying the principles above. Rotating between them every few months keeps training fresh and teaches you what your body responds to.

Science-based hypertrophy

Who
Eric Helms & 3DMJ, Jeff Nippard, Stronger By Science, Milo Wolf
Idea
Moderate volume, sets near failure, exercises chosen for stretch and stability, steady double progression. The closest thing to a “default” for natural bodybuilding.
Try
Helms’ Pyramid intermediate program; Nippard’s Pure Bodybuilding (push-pull-legs plus an arm and weak-point day, 2–4 sets of 8–15 with RPE targets).

Renaissance Periodization (RP)

Who
Dr. Mike Israetel, James Hoffmann
Idea
“Volume landmarks”: start each 4–6 week block near the least volume that grows you, add sets weekly as recovery allows, then deload. Rate pump, soreness and fatigue to decide.
Try
A 5-week block: start at ~10 sets per muscle, add 1–2 sets a week, deload in week 5.

Barbell novice progression

Who
Mark Rippetoe (Starting Strength)
Idea
Squat, press, deadlift; 3 sets of 5; add weight every session until you can’t. Fast early strength, little isolation work.
Try
Good for a strength reset or after a long layoff. Their lift tutorials (below) are worth watching regardless.

5/3/1

Who
Jim Wendler
Idea
Work from a training max (90% of your 1-rep max). Three-week waves — 65/75/85%, 70/80/90%, 75/85/95% — with the last set for max reps, then a deload. Slow, sustainable, hard to stall.
Try
Boring But Big: 4 days a week, main lift plus 5 sets of 10 at 50–60% for size.

GZCL

Who
Cody LeFever
Idea
Three tiers each day: T1 heavy (e.g. 5×3+), T2 lighter compound (3×10), T3 accessories (3×15+). Roughly 1:2:3 reps across tiers — strength and size together.
Try
GZCLP (3 days) for linear progress; Jacked & Tan 2.0 for intermediates.

RPE & autoregulation

Who
Mike Tuchscherer (RTS), Barbell Medicine
Idea
Prescribe effort, not fixed weights: “3 reps at RPE 8” means a weight you could do 5 with. Load moves up and down with how you feel that day.
Try
Add RPE targets to your top sets in any program.

Minimalist strength

Who
Dan John, Pavel Tsatsouline (StrongFirst)
Idea
Few lifts, done often, never to failure. Easy Strength: same 15–20 minute workout 5 days a week for 8 weeks — hinge, press, pull 2×5, swings, one core set. Simple & Sinister: kettlebell swings and get-ups almost daily.
Try
Busy seasons, or as a strength base alongside sports.

High-intensity training (HIT)

Who
Mike Mentzer (Heavy Duty), Dorian Yates
Idea
One or two all-out sets per exercise, infrequent sessions. Time-efficient; the volume research suggests it leaves growth on the table for most people.
Try
A short block when time is very tight.

Calisthenics

Who
Steven Low (Overcoming Gravity), FitnessFAQs, r/bodyweightfitness
Idea
Progressions instead of plates — harder leverage each step. The Recommended Routine: 3 days a week, paired pull/squat, dip/hinge, row/push-up, 3 sets of 5–8.
Try
Pull-ups, dips and a handstand practice pair well with any lifting program and with backbend work.

Bigger Leaner Stronger

Who
Mike Matthews
Idea
Heavy compound lifting in lower rep ranges, simple progression, flexible dieting. A solid base; branching out adds more volume, more stretched-position isolation work and different rep ranges.

A rotation to try: 12 weeks of the upper/lower program below → 12 weeks of 5/3/1 Boring But Big → 8 weeks of GZCL or an RP-style block. Test your main lifts at the end of each.

A 4-day upper/lower program

Science-based hypertrophy with heavy compounds · about 10–14 sets per muscle per week · fits the sample week on the Hub

Use double progression on everything. Compounds stop 1–2 reps short of failure; isolation work goes to 0–1 short.

Upper A — Monday

  1. Bench press — 3 × 5–8
  2. Chest-supported row — 3 × 8–12
  3. Standing overhead press — 2 × 6–10
  4. Lat pulldown — 3 × 10–15
  5. Lateral raise — 3 × 12–20
  6. Overhead triceps extension — 2 × 10–15
  7. Incline dumbbell curl — 2 × 10–15

Lower A — Tuesday

  1. Back squat — 3 × 5–8
  2. Romanian deadlift — 3 × 6–10
  3. Leg press, deep — 2 × 10–15
  4. Seated leg curl — 3 × 10–15
  5. Standing calf raise, 2-second pause at the bottom — 3 × 10–15
  6. Hanging leg raise — 2 × 10–15

Upper B — Thursday

  1. Incline dumbbell press — 3 × 8–12
  2. Pull-up (add weight once you pass 12) — 3 × 6–12
  3. Seated cable row — 3 × 10–15
  4. Seated dumbbell shoulder press — 2 × 8–12
  5. Cable or dumbbell fly, deep stretch — 2 × 12–15
  6. Lateral raise — 3 × 12–20
  7. Triceps pushdown — 2 × 10–15
  8. Hammer curl — 2 × 10–15

Lower B — Friday

  1. Deadlift — 3 × 3–5
  2. Bulgarian split squat — 3 × 8–12 per leg
  3. Leg extension — 3 × 12–15
  4. Lying leg curl — 2 × 10–15
  5. Hip thrust — 2 × 8–12
  6. Seated calf raise — 3 × 12–20

Warm up each main lift with 2–4 lighter ramp-up sets. Swap any exercise that hurts for one that trains the same muscle.

Natural bodybuilding

Realistic expectations

  • The first year is the fastest. Gains slow every year after, so patience and consistency matter more than any program detail.
  • FFMI (fat-free mass ÷ height² in kg/m²) is a common yardstick. A 1995 study found non-users averaged about 22 and steroid users about 25; a few naturals reach 25+, and the high 20s may be possible for a rare few.
  • Martin Berkhan’s rule of thumb for a stage-lean natural ceiling: height in cm minus about 100, in kilograms. At 6′1″ (185 cm) that’s roughly 85 kg (187 lb) at contest leanness — heavier at everyday body fat.

Bulking and cutting

  • Lean bulk: a 10–20% calorie surplus, gaining 0.25–0.5% of bodyweight per week. More advanced lifters should gain more slowly.
  • Cut: lose 0.5–1% of bodyweight per week; keep training heavy and protein high.
  • Details and a calculator are on the Nutrition page.

Balanced, symmetric strength

Compare your lifts against each other, not just against bodyweight. Symmetric Strength scores each lift and shows which ones lag; Strength Level ranks a lift against millions of logged lifts by bodyweight and age. Bring up the weakest lift with an extra 2–4 sets a week.

Staying energetic while training hard

  • Volume you can’t recover from shows up as all-day fatigue before it shows up in the gym. Cut sets before you cut sleep.
  • Train at a consistent time; earlier sessions protect sleep.
  • Eat carbs around training and enough total food — a steep deficit is the most common energy killer.
