What the research agrees on

Programs look different on the surface, but the good ones share the same core.

Volume 10–20 hard sets per muscle per week covers most people.[1] A 2025 meta-analysis of 67 studies found growth from as little as 4 weekly sets, with more sets still helping but each extra set adding less — and little data above about 25.[2]
Effort Stopping closer to failure builds more muscle; strength gains are similar across a wide range of effort.[3] Practical rule: big compound lifts 1–3 reps short of failure, isolation lifts 0–2.[1]
Frequency Each muscle about twice a week beats once.[4] Split a muscle’s weekly sets across more days as volume rises.[1]
Reps Light and heavy loads build similar muscle when sets are taken close to failure; heavier loads build more strength.[5] Use 6–15 reps for most hypertrophy work and lower reps for strength.[1]
Progression Add reps, then weight. Double progression: e.g. 3 sets of 10–15 — once every set hits the top of the range, add weight.[1]
Range Full range of motion, with emphasis on the stretched position (deep squats, seated leg curls, deep-stretch flyes). A recent review found long-muscle-length training may beat short-length training for growth, though the evidence is mixed.[6]
RestLonger rests (around 3 minutes) beat 1 minute for both strength and size in trained men; 2–3 minutes for compounds, 1–2 for isolation is a practical default.[7]
Deloads Reactive rather than scheduled. Each week, ask: dreading training? sleeping worse? loads or reps falling? aches worse than usual? Two or more yeses — take a lighter week.[1]

Schools of thought

Each one is a different way of applying the principles above. Rotating between them every few months keeps training fresh and teaches you what your body responds to.

Science-based hypertrophy

Who
Eric Helms & 3DMJ, Jeff Nippard, Stronger By Science, Milo Wolf (Muscle Lab)
Idea
Moderate volume, sets near failure, exercises chosen for stretch and stability, steady double progression. The closest thing to a “default” for natural bodybuilding.
Try
Helms’ Pyramid intermediate program; Nippard’s Pure Bodybuilding — push-pull-legs plus an arm and weak-point day, 2–4 sets of 8–15 with RPE targets.[8]

Renaissance Periodization (RP)

Who
Dr. Mike Israetel, James Hoffmann — rpstrength.com
Idea
“Volume landmarks”: start each 4–6 week block near the least volume that grows you, add sets weekly as recovery allows, then deload. Rate pump, soreness and fatigue to decide.[9]
Try
A 5-week block: start at ~10 sets per muscle, add 1–2 sets a week, deload in week 5. Full detail in Scientific Principles of Hypertrophy Training.

Barbell novice progression

Who
Mark Rippetoe — startingstrength.com
Idea
Squat, press, deadlift; 3 sets of 5; add weight every session until you can’t. Fast early strength, little isolation work.
Try
Good for a strength reset or after a long layoff. The lift tutorials below are worth watching regardless.

5/3/1

Who
Jim Wendler — 5/3/1 Forever
Idea
Work from a training max (90% of your 1-rep max). Three-week waves — 65/75/85%, 70/80/90%, 75/85/95% — with the last set for max reps, then a deload.[10]
Try
Boring But Big: 4 days a week, main lift plus 5 sets of 10 at 50–60% for size.[11]

GZCL

Who
Cody LeFever
Idea
Three tiers each day: T1 heavy (e.g. 5×3+), T2 lighter compound (3×10), T3 accessories (3×15+). Roughly 1:2:3 reps across tiers — strength and size together.[12, 13]
Try
GZCLP (3 days) for linear progress; Jacked & Tan 2.0 for intermediates.[13]

RPE & autoregulation

Who
Mike Tuchscherer (Reactive Training Systems), Barbell Medicine
Idea
Prescribe effort, not fixed weights: “3 reps at RPE 8” means a weight you could do 5 with. Load moves up and down with how you feel that day.[14]
Try
Add RPE targets to your top sets in any program.

Minimalist strength

Who
Dan John, Pavel Tsatsouline (StrongFirst)
Idea
Few lifts, done often, never to failure. Easy Strength: the same 15–20 minute workout 5 days a week for 8 weeks — hinge, press, pull 2×5, swings, one core set.[15] Simple & Sinister: kettlebell swings and get-ups most days.[16]
Try
Busy seasons, or as a strength base alongside sports.

High-intensity training (HIT)

Who
Mike Mentzer (Heavy Duty), Dorian Yates
Idea
One or two all-out sets per exercise, infrequent sessions.[17] Time-efficient; the volume research suggests it leaves growth on the table for most people.[2]
Try
A short block when time is very tight.

Calisthenics

Who
Steven Low (Overcoming Gravity), FitnessFAQs, r/bodyweightfitness
Idea
Progressions instead of plates — harder leverage each step. The Recommended Routine: 3 days a week, paired pull/squat, dip/hinge, row/push-up, 3 sets of 5–8.[18]
Try
Pull-ups, dips and handstand practice pair well with any lifting program and with backbend work.

Bigger Leaner Stronger

Who
Mike Matthews — Bigger Leaner Stronger
Idea
Heavy compound lifting in lower rep ranges, simple progression, flexible dieting. A solid base; branching out adds more volume, more stretched-position isolation work and different rep ranges.

A rotation to try: 12 weeks of the upper/lower program below → 12 weeks of 5/3/1 Boring But Big → 8 weeks of GZCL or an RP-style block. Test your main lifts at the end of each.

A 4-day upper/lower program

Built on the principles above · about 10–14 sets per muscle per week · fits the sample week on the Hub

Use double progression on everything. Compounds stop 1–2 reps short of failure; isolation work goes to 0–1 short.

Upper A — Monday

  1. Bench press — 3 × 5–8
  2. Chest-supported row — 3 × 8–12
  3. Standing overhead press — 2 × 6–10
  4. Lat pulldown — 3 × 10–15
  5. Lateral raise — 3 × 12–20
  6. Overhead triceps extension — 2 × 10–15
  7. Incline dumbbell curl — 2 × 10–15

Lower A — Tuesday

  1. Back squat — 3 × 5–8
  2. Romanian deadlift — 3 × 6–10
  3. Leg press, deep — 2 × 10–15
  4. Seated leg curl — 3 × 10–15
  5. Standing calf raise, 2-second pause at the bottom — 3 × 10–15
  6. Hanging leg raise — 2 × 10–15

Upper B — Thursday

  1. Incline dumbbell press — 3 × 8–12
  2. Pull-up (add weight once you pass 12) — 3 × 6–12
  3. Seated cable row — 3 × 10–15
  4. Seated dumbbell shoulder press — 2 × 8–12
  5. Cable or dumbbell fly, deep stretch — 2 × 12–15
  6. Lateral raise — 3 × 12–20
  7. Triceps pushdown — 2 × 10–15
  8. Hammer curl — 2 × 10–15

Lower B — Friday

  1. Deadlift — 3 × 3–5
  2. Bulgarian split squat — 3 × 8–12 per leg
  3. Leg extension — 3 × 12–15
  4. Lying leg curl — 2 × 10–15
  5. Hip thrust — 2 × 8–12
  6. Seated calf raise — 3 × 12–20

Warm up each main lift with 2–4 lighter ramp-up sets. Swap any exercise that hurts for one that trains the same muscle. Free spreadsheets for this and most programs named above are on Lift Vault.

Natural bodybuilding

Realistic expectations

  • The first year is the fastest; gains slow every year after, so consistency matters more than any program detail.[19]
  • FFMI (fat-free mass ÷ height² in kg/m²) is a common yardstick. A 1995 study found non-users averaged about 22 and steroid users about 25; a few naturals reach 25+, and the high 20s may be possible for a rare few.[19]
  • Martin Berkhan’s rule of thumb for a stage-lean natural ceiling: height in cm minus about 100, in kilograms. At 6′1″ (185 cm) that’s roughly 85 kg (187 lb) at contest leanness — heavier at everyday body fat.[19]

Bulking and cutting

  • Lean bulk: a 10–20% calorie surplus, gaining 0.25–0.5% of bodyweight per week; more advanced lifters should gain more slowly.[20]
  • Cut: lose 0.5–1% of bodyweight per week; keep training heavy and protein high.[21]
  • Details and a calculator are on the Nutrition page.

Balanced, symmetric strength

Compare your lifts against each other, not just against bodyweight. Symmetric Strength scores each lift and shows which ones lag; Strength Level ranks a lift against millions of logged lifts by bodyweight and age.[22] Bring up the weakest lift with an extra 2–4 sets a week.

Staying energetic while training hard

  • General fatigue is the most common early sign of doing more than you can recover from.[23] Cut sets before you cut sleep.
  • Train at a consistent time; earlier sessions protect sleep.
  • Eat carbs around training and enough total food — a steep, long deficit is a common energy killer.[21]
  • More on the Energy & Health page.
